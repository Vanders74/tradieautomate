Getting the apprentice ratio wrong is not a paperwork problem. It is a breach of the supervision requirements made under your state's electrical safety legislation, it can suspend or cancel a licence (in NSW it is a condition of the electrician's licence itself), and in a serious incident it exposes the business and the supervising electrician personally. Underpayment is a separate problem again, enforced under the **Fair Work Act 2009**.

**An apprentice ratio is the maximum number of apprentices one licensed electrician may supervise at the same time, at a given level of supervision.** It is a per-supervisor cap, not a headcount formula for the business — which is why the old "one apprentice per tradesperson, scaling 2:2 and 3:3" rule of thumb fails in most states.

This guide maps each state's actual supervision ratios, the federal incentives available in 2026, state-specific training subsidies, and the real net cost of hiring your first electrical apprentice.

## Why Ratios Exist — and Who Enforces Them

Apprentice supervision ratios exist because an apprentice working without adequate supervision is a safety risk in a licensed trade — and the completion data backs that up. NCVER's 2025 completion study puts the six-year completion rate for electricians at **71.7%**, the best of the ten biggest trades, but four-year attrition across trade apprenticeships in the 2021 commencing cohort was **42.1%**, the highest on record. Read those two numbers together and the story is retention over time, not mass walkout: a lot of the apparent loss is apprentices still training at the four-year mark, and the ones who leave mostly leave early.

There are two separate rulebooks, and conflating them is the most common mistake:

1. **Electrical safety law** sets the ratios. In most states these are now tiered by supervision level — direct (constant attendance), general (supervisor on site and available) and broad (occasional face-to-face contact) — and the caps differ at each level. Enforced by the state electrical safety regulator, not a training authority.
2. **Training contract law** requires the apprentice to be supervised in line with the training plan and the qualification's on-the-job requirements. Enforced by the state skills authority that registers the contract.

Fair Work's jurisdiction is narrower than most employers assume. The Fair Work Act and MA000025 govern wages, superannuation and employment classification — apprentice minimum rates, allowances, overtime. They do not set supervision ratios.

The regulators that actually enforce ratios:

- **NSW:** Building Commission NSW (Supervision Practice Standard)
- **VIC:** Energy Safe Victoria (Apprentice Supervision Requirements, in force 1 September 2025)
- **QLD:** Electrical Safety Office, WorkSafe Queensland (Electrical Safety Act 2002)
- **SA:** Consumer and Business Services for electrical licensing, the Office of the Technical Regulator for electrical safety, and the South Australian Skills Commission for the supervision ratio itself (Traineeship and Apprenticeship Pathways Schedule)
- **WA:** Building and Energy, Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety
- **TAS:** Consumer, Building and Occupational Services (CBOS), under the Occupational Licensing (Supervision of Prescribed Work) Code of Practice 2022 — WorkSafe Tasmania covers WHS, not the ratio
- **ACT:** WorkSafe ACT
- **NT:** NT WorkSafe (Electrical Safety Regulations 2024)

## State-by-State Apprentice Ratio Table

Ratios below are per supervisor, per supervision level. "Direct" means constant attendance; "general" means the supervisor is on site and available; "broad" means occasional face-to-face contact to check the work.

| State | Direct | General | Broad | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NSW | 1:1 | 1:3 | 1:5 | Supervision Practice Standard (Building Commission NSW), a condition of the electrician's licence since 1 Sep 2024 |
| VIC | 1:1 | 1:3 | 1:5 | Apprentice Supervision Requirements, effective 1 Sep 2025 |
| QLD | No numeric cap — effective supervision only | — | — | Electrical Safety Act 2002; ESO *Supervising electrical apprentices* |
| SA | Not tiered by supervision level. The qualification itself is risk-rated: **high 1:3, medium 1:6, low 1:10** | | | SA Skills Commission, Traineeship and Apprenticeship Pathways Schedule |
| WA | 1:2 max, and only where at least one of the two needs direct supervision | 1:4 is the absolute ceiling in any case | 1:4 is the absolute ceiling in any case | Safe working guidelines, Electricity (Licensing) Regulations 1991 |
| TAS | 1:1, adjustable after a formal risk assessment | Set by risk assessment | Set by risk assessment | Occupational Licensing (Supervision of Prescribed Work) Code of Practice 2022 |
| ACT | 1:1 (years 1–2) | 1:5 (years 3–4) | — | WorkSafe ACT apprentice supervision guidance |
| NT | 1:1 | 1:3 (general level 1) | 1:5 (general level 2) | Electrical Safety Regulations 2024 |

**Key rules that apply everywhere:**

- **The cap is per supervisor, per moment** — not per business. Two licensed electricians on site do not double one supervisor's limit unless both are genuinely supervising.
- **Supervision level tracks competence, not just year of apprenticeship.** In NSW and Victoria the move from direct to general to broad depends on an assessment of the individual apprentice's competence for that task, and combination limits apply (for example, one apprentice under direct plus one under general).
- **The ratio only bites on licensed electrical installation work.** In Victoria the requirements apply when the apprentice is doing electrical installation work as defined in the Electricity Safety Act 1998 — not to sweeping the van or stacking conduit. Queensland draws the same line differently: an apprentice with less than six months' training cannot work in the immediate vicinity of live parts at all, testing included, and must be constantly supervised.
- **Victoria's numbers changed in September 2025, and most trade forums still quote the old ones.** Direct moved from 1:2 to 1:1, general from 1:4 to 1:3, and broad went from undefined to 1:5. If you are working from a 2024 supervision guide, you are over your cap. Victoria also bars first-year apprentices from fault-finding until at least year three.
- **SA works differently from every other state.** The 1:3 / 1:6 / 1:10 figures attach to the *qualification's* risk rating, not to a supervision level, and the ratio is set by the Skills Commission, not the electrical regulator. Exceeding it needs written approval from the Commission, which you must then keep on site. SA also recognises direct, indirect and remote supervision — and prohibits remote supervision outright without written approval.
- **A licensed subcontractor does not count toward your ratio** unless they are directly engaged by your business and actually supervising.

## Group Training Organisations — The Alternative for Small Shops

A sole trader is capped at what one supervisor can supervise — often a single apprentice under direct supervision. A **Group Training Organisation (GTO)** is the workaround: the GTO employs the apprentice and places them with you as host, carrying the employment, payroll and training-contract obligations. You pay a weekly rate covering wages, on-costs and the GTO margin.

Under the GTO model:
- The GTO is the legal employer of the apprentice
- The GTO places the apprentice with your business as a host employer
- The GTO manages payroll, super, training contracts, and ratio compliance
- You pay the GTO a weekly rate (covering wages + on-costs + GTO margin)

**The trade-off:** GTOs cost slightly more per week than direct employment once you factor in their margin. The benefit is zero administrative complexity and no ratio compliance risk. For a sole trader who wants an apprentice but can't always guarantee they'll have a second qualified tradesperson on-site, a GTO is often the right call.

Find registered GTOs through the Australian Apprenticeships website (australianapprenticeships.gov.au).

## Federal Incentive Payments — Australian Apprenticeships Incentive System (AAIS)

The **Australian Apprenticeships Incentive System** is the federal framework for employer and apprentice payments. Its settings changed on 1 January 2026 and are now built around two employer payments plus the Key Apprenticeship Program. If you are working from older material quoting a $3,500 hiring incentive or a separate recommencement payment, that is the pre-2024 scheme — those amounts now apply only to apprentices who commenced or recommenced before 1 July 2024 under grandfathering.

**The deadline that decides what you get:** the $5,000 Key Apprenticeship Program Employer Incentive is the 2026 rate. For apprenticeships that start on or after **1 January 2027** it drops to **$4,000**, and employers with 200 or more employees stop being eligible altogether (Group Training Organisations excepted). Anything that commences before 1 January 2027 keeps the $5,000. If you are deciding whether to sign a training contract this quarter or next, that is a $1,000 difference for the same apprentice.

### Employer Incentives (2026 settings)

**Key Apprenticeship Program Employer Incentive — up to $5,000**
- Available for: employers of apprentices in a Certificate III or higher qualification identified as **New Energy** or **Housing Construction** on the Australian Apprenticeships Priority List
- Amount: up to **$5,000** in year one — $2,000 at 6 months and $3,000 at 12 months (full-time); $1,000 and $1,500 part-time
- The catch: your business must give the apprentice genuine exposure and work in the clean energy or housing construction sector. An electrical contractor doing rooftop solar, battery installs or EV charging meets this. A general maintenance sparky whose apprentice only ever sees domestic fault-finding generally does not
- You sign a declaration confirming the sector work, so this is not a box you can tick loosely

**Priority Hiring Incentive — up to $2,500**
- Available for: employers of apprentices training towards a Certificate III or above on the Priority List who are not in a Key Apprenticeship Program stream
- Amount: up to **$2,500** in year one — $1,000 at 6 months and $1,500 at 12 months (full-time); $500 and $750 part-time
- You cannot receive this and the Key Apprenticeship Program Employer Incentive for the same apprentice

**Grandfathered payments (commenced before 1 July 2024)**
- Hiring Incentive: $1,750 full-time, paid across the 6 and 12-month milestones
- Support for Adult Australian Apprentices: $4,000 where the apprentice was 21 or over at commencement
- These do not apply to new 2026 commencements

### The payment to the apprentice — $10,000, and the best recruiting line you have

The page above is all employer money, but the incentive that actually gets a 17-year-old to sign is the **Key Apprenticeship Program Apprentice Incentive: up to $10,000** over the life of a full-time apprenticeship ($5,000 part-time), paid in $2,000 instalments at 6, 12, 24 and 36 months and on completion. It is available in the same New Energy and Housing Construction streams as the employer payment, for apprenticeships that commenced or recommenced after 1 July 2025.

Two things follow from that. The apprentice payment is paid to the apprentice, not offset against your wage bill — so it is a recruitment and retention tool, not a cost reduction. And the milestone structure is deliberately built to keep them to completion, which is the same problem the 42.1% four-year attrition number above describes. Say the number in the job ad; almost none of your competitors do.

### Claiming — and who your provider is now

The Australian Apprenticeships Support Network (AASN) was replaced on 1 July 2024. Your provider is now an **Apprentice Connect Australia Provider**, and you must be registered with one when the training contract is signed. Claims are lodged through the **Apprenticeships Data Management System (ADMS)**. Your provider handles the paperwork, but the eligibility declarations are yours.

## State-Level Extras: What Each State Adds

### NSW — Fee-Free Training
Under the NSW fee-free apprenticeship initiative, apprentices pay no upfront course fee — the government covers the student fee of up to roughly $2,000 a year under Smart and Skilled, at TAFE NSW and other Smart and Skilled providers. It applies to training commencing before 30 June 2027, so a contract signed in 2026 is covered for its duration.

### VIC — Big Build Apprenticeships
Victorian Skills Authority runs the **Big Build Apprenticeships** program, focused on construction and infrastructure trades. Electrical apprentices working on eligible infrastructure projects may access additional wage subsidies. Check current program eligibility at skills.vic.gov.au.

### QLD — Free Apprenticeships for Under 25s
The Queensland Government provides **free TAFE and RTO training** for apprentices under 25 in priority trades, delivered through TAFE Queensland and other registered providers. Electrical (UEE30820) is on the priority list. This eliminates training fees for the most common age demographic for new electrical apprentices.

### SA — TAFE SA Subsidies
South Australia provides subsidised training through TAFE SA for electrical apprentices. Confirm current subsidy rates with the SA Department for Education, as rates vary by apprenticeship year and qualification.

## Real Cost Breakdown: What an Electrical Apprentice Actually Costs

The gross cost of an electrical apprentice in 2026 under the Electrical, Electronic and Communications Contracting Award MA000025. The award sets apprentice pay as a percentage of the **Electrical worker grade 5** rate, which is $1,119.10 a week ($29.45 an hour) from 1 July 2026 — but the percentage is applied to grade 5 **plus** the industry and licence allowances, and the full tool allowance is then added. That is why the published weekly rates below are higher than a straight percentage of $1,119.10, and why you must not add the allowances a second time:

| Year | % Grade 5 (no Year 12) | % Grade 5 (Year 12) | Weekly rate, no Year 12 | Weekly rate, Year 12 | Annual (no Year 12) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 50% | 55% | $622.72 | $682.76 | ~$32,400 |
| Year 2 | 60% | 65% | $742.79 | $802.84 | ~$38,600 |
| Year 3 | 70% | 70% | $862.87 | $862.87 | ~$44,900 |
| Year 4 | 82% | 82% | $1,006.97 | $1,006.97 | ~$52,400 |

*These are the Fair Work pay guide rates effective the first full pay period on or after 1 July 2026, and they already include the full $22.31/week tool allowance and the apprentice's share of the $41.41 industry allowance and $40.29 electrician's licence allowance. They are the post-1 January 2014 apprentice percentages; apprentices who started before that date sit on the older 40%/52%/70%/82% scale, which Fair Work says to calculate through its Pay and Conditions Tool. Rates rise each 1 July.*

**Adult apprentices (21 or over) are on a separate, much higher scale:** first year $982.95 a week ($25.87/hour), then $1,098.29 a week for years two to four — roughly $15,000 a year more than a school leaver in year one. If you are hiring a mature-age apprentice, budget off those numbers, not the junior table.

**On-costs on top of wages:**
- Superannuation at 12% (the rate from 1 July 2025): ~$3,900 in year one
- Workers compensation insurance: varies by state, typically 3–6% of wages for electrical
- Long service leave accrual: roughly 1.67% of wages
- Uniforms and PPE: $500–$1,500/year

**Total annual employment cost (Year 1, no Year 12):** approximately $38,000–$40,000. Add roughly $3,700 if the apprentice completed Year 12.

**Less Key Apprenticeship Program Employer Incentive (clean energy work):** -$5,000

**Net Year 1 cost (before state training subsidies):** approximately $33,000–$35,000

*If you only qualify for the $2,500 Priority Hiring Incentive, net year-one cost is roughly $35,500–$37,500.*

One arithmetic trap worth naming, because it is the most common error on Australian trade sites: the allowances are often quoted as an add-on to the apprentice percentage, which double-counts them. A first-year on $622.72 is already being paid the tool, industry and licence allowances; adding "another $60–$70 a week" overstates the wage bill by about $3,400 a year and makes the whole hiring decision look worse than it is.

## The 4-Year Trade: What You Get in Return

An electrical apprenticeship under UEE30820 — Certificate III in Electrotechnology Electrician — runs for 4 years. By the end:

- You have a fully licensed electrician, trained in your methods and systems
- Years 3 and 4 are where you see meaningful productivity — a Year 4 apprentice can run small jobs independently under supervision
- Staff retention post-trade is significantly higher for apprentices you've trained than for tradesperson hires
- Your business has demonstrated capacity to train, which opens doors to GTO and government contract work

The real payback is in Year 3 and beyond. Years 1 and 2 are a net cost. Model the investment accordingly.
