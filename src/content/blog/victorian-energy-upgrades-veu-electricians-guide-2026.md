> **Victorian Energy Efficiency Certificate (VEEC):** A tradeable certificate created under the Victorian Energy Upgrades (VEU) program when an accredited party completes an eligible energy efficiency upgrade. Each VEEC represents one tonne of greenhouse gas abatement. Certificates are traded on the open market and purchased by obligated retailers to meet their annual scheme targets.

Victorian electricians doing heat pump hot water installations, lighting upgrades, and gas-to-electric space heating replacements are sitting on a revenue stream most aren't claiming. The Victorian Energy Upgrades (VEU) program — established under the **Victorian Energy Efficiency Target Act 2007 (VEET Act)** and administered by the **Essential Services Commission (ESC)** — lets accredited persons create **Victorian Energy Efficiency Certificates (VEECs)** after completing eligible upgrades. Those certificates are then sold on the open market.

**What a VEEC is worth right now.** VEECs are a traded commodity, not a fixed rebate, and the spot price moves week to week. In the week to 16 October 2026 the spot closed at **$83.25**, with late-2026 forwards around $83. That is down from a peak near **$96 in mid-September**, and the fall has a cause worth understanding: the ESC opened **Activity 47 (commercial and industrial solar)** on 1 October, the market expects large certificate volumes from it, and the 2026 scheme target was cut from 7.3 million certificates to 4.4 million. More expected supply plus a smaller target is a lower price. Budget at the low end of the recent range, not the middle. (This page previously quoted $25–$55 — that was the price two years earlier, and it understated every number below by a factor of two or more.)

The certificate count is the variable you control; the price is the one you don't. At $83, a job that creates 8 VEECs is worth about $665 and a job that creates 20 is worth about $1,660. The count depends on the activity, the approved product, the postcode's climate zone and what is being replaced — the ESC's activity calculator in the VEU Registry gives the exact figure. Run it before you quote, not after.

Two sanity checks before you put a number in front of a customer. The Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action advertises discounts of **up to $630 on heat pump hot water**, up to $5,530 on reverse-cycle air conditioning and up to $34,300 on a 200 kW commercial solar system. Those are the published programme maxima for consumers. If your calculated discount is several times the advertised maximum for that product, you have over-counted certificates, not discovered a deal.

This guide explains how the VEU program works for electricians — who can participate, what activities qualify, how to create certificates, and how VEU interacts with federal STCs on the same property.

**What is a VEEC?** A Victorian Energy Efficiency Certificate is a tradeable certificate representing one tonne of greenhouse gas abatement, created by an accredited person after completing an eligible activity under the VEET Regulations 2018. Certificates are sold to energy retailers to meet annual scheme targets — generating revenue on top of installation fees.

## How the VEU Program Works

The VEU program creates a market-based mechanism for reducing energy consumption across Victoria. Established under the **Victorian Energy Efficiency Target Act 2007**, with eligible activities defined in the **Victorian Energy Upgrades Specifications 2018** and the VEET Regulations, the scheme obligates electricity and gas retailers with a liability under the program to surrender VEECs each year to meet targets set by the government. They buy VEECs from the entities that create them. The program is legislated to continue until 2045.

The flow looks like this:

1. An accredited person completes — or has a registered scheme participant complete — an eligible energy efficiency upgrade
2. The accredited person creates VEECs in the VEU Registry, supported by evidence of the installation
3. VEECs are sold (directly to retailers, via brokers, or through aggregators) at the prevailing spot price
4. Retailers surrender VEECs to meet their annual obligation

**Three terms that get used interchangeably, and shouldn't be.** The ESC draws a hard line between them:

- **Accredited person** — the entity accredited by the ESC that creates VEECs. DEECA's installer pages call the same role an **accredited provider**. This is the one holding the program liability.
- **Scheme participant** — an installer, assessor or lighting designer *registered by an accredited person* to carry out the work. The accredited person must register you as a scheme participant before it can submit activities you performed.
- **Liable entity** — the energy retailer that has to surrender certificates.

For electricians, participation works one of two ways:

**Option A — Become the accredited person yourself.** You apply to the ESC, get approved for specific activity types, and create and trade VEECs directly. You carry the compliance obligations, the audits and the record-keeping, but you capture the certificate value rather than a fixed fee.

**Option B — Get registered as a scheme participant under someone else's accreditation.** Most electricians start here. An accredited person or aggregator recruits you, registers you as a scheme participant in the VEU Registry, and handles certificate creation and evidence compliance. You do the installation and get paid; the customer gets a point-of-sale discount the accredited person finances.

For a reasonably active electrical business doing 20+ eligible installs per month, Option A can deliver better economics once you have the admin capacity. For sole traders or businesses just testing the model, Option B is the lower-friction starting point — and it is not a lesser status: the work is still yours, the licensing requirement is identical.

## What VEU Accreditation Actually Requires — and What It Costs

The published accreditation tests are narrower than most guides suggest, and the fees matter far more than the paperwork.

**To apply to become an accredited person you need:**

- A **VEU account**, opened on the ESC's registry with your **ABN** and business details
- A complete online accreditation application, referencing the activities you want
- The **$3,000 accreditation application fee**, paid once the ESC invoices you
- To pass the ESC's **fit and proper** and **competent and capable** tests — these are re-tested every year you remain accredited
- **Evidence of insurance**, including a published minimum public liability figure of **$5 million** for every activity except commercial and industrial solar PV, which requires **$10 million**

That's the list. Two things worth spelling out, because most guides get one or both wrong. First, the licence: a trade licence on its own does not get you accredited, but it is not optional either — the ESC asks applicants for evidence of the licensing and regulatory approvals that attach to the activities they want, and electrician and plumbing licences are named in its application guide as that evidence. Second, insurance is an accreditation condition with hard numbers, not a commercial convention: the Application Guide's minimum insurance table sets **public liability at $5M** (and **product liability at $5M**) for every prescribed activity except C&I solar (activity 47), where both rise to **$10M**, plus **$5M professional indemnity** where the accredited person gives advice on gas efficiency, cold rooms, commercial heat pump water heaters, home energy rating assessments, C&I solar or project-based activities.

Every electrical installation under a VEU activity must also be carried out by a licensed electrician registered with Energy Safe Victoria — that obligation attaches to the *work* regardless of who holds the accreditation. And for scheme participants working under an accredited person's registration, the ESC reviews and approves the registration for **activities 13, 14, 15, 17, 26, 45, 47 and 48**, which means submitting proof of the participant's licence, training and competency before their work can be claimed.

**Fees, from 1 January 2026 (ESC schedule):**

| Fee | Amount |
|---|---|
| Application for accreditation | $3,000 |
| Annual renewal of accreditation | $1,000 |
| Late renewal (lodged under 90 days before expiry) | $7,376 |
| Variation of accreditation conditions (adding activities) | $1,500 |
| Creation and registration of each VEEC | $4.35 |
| Opening a VEET scheme registry account | $2,000 (waived for accredited persons in many cases) |
| Request for reconsideration of a reviewable decision | $750 |

**The term and the renewal trap.** Accreditation runs for **12 months** from the date it is granted, not indefinitely. Renewal must be lodged at least **90 calendar days** before the expiry date; inside that window the $7,376 late fee applies, and if you don't renew at all you drop out of the program and re-applying costs the full $3,000 again. Diarise the expiry 120 days out, not 90.

**Adding activities later.** Accreditation is activity-specific — approval for heat pump hot water does not extend to lighting or appliances. Adding an activity is a variation application ($1,500), and you can only create VEECs for the added activity from the date the commission approves it and your new accreditation period begins.

**Ongoing obligations that catch people out:**

- **Independent assurance audit at least once every two years**, using an auditor from the ESC's approved list and paid for by you — plus compliance audits on request
- **Records kept until the end of six years** after they were made (or after the transactions they relate to are completed, whichever is later), including the evidence that substantiates the activity
- Registration of every installer, assessor or lighting designer as a **scheme participant** before their work is submitted

Start from the ESC's program pages — [how to become a VEU accredited person](https://www.esc.vic.gov.au/victorian-energy-upgrades-program/participating-veu-program/how-become-veu-accredited-person) and the [VEU program fees](https://www.esc.vic.gov.au/victorian-energy-upgrades/about-victorian-energy-upgrades-program/veu-program-fees) — then read the current activity guide for the specific activity before you quote a single job.

## Eligible Activities in 2026

The VEU program covers a wide range of energy efficiency upgrades. The most commercially relevant for electricians in 2026 are:

### 1. Heat Pump Hot Water Systems

The highest-volume activity for most participating electricians. When replacing an existing electric storage or gas hot water system with an eligible heat pump unit, VEECs are calculated based on the energy savings the new system delivers over its rated life.

**Key requirements:**
- Product must be on the ESC's approved product list (updated regularly — check before quoting)
- Must replace an existing system (new installations on properties with no prior hot water don't qualify)
- Photographic evidence required: existing system, new installation, serial numbers
- Commissioning documentation required

**VEEC quantity:** Varies by approved product, postcode climate zone and the system being replaced — and the activity factors themselves are adjusted from time to time. The calculator in the VEU Registry is the only figure worth quoting; don't carry a number from any guide, including this one, into a customer's quote.

### 2. Residential and Commercial Lighting Upgrades

Replacing existing inefficient lighting (halogen, T8 fluorescent, metal halide) with LED equivalents generates VEECs based on the energy reduction per luminaire per year.

**Key requirements:**
- Must be a replacement (not new installation)
- Products must meet minimum efficacy thresholds
- Commercial lighting upgrades require additional documentation (metered hours of use, floor area)
- Large-scale commercial lighting projects can generate significant VEEC volumes — 100-bay warehouse lighting upgrade can yield 500+ VEECs

### 3. Space Heating Replacements (Gas-to-Electric)

With the push toward electrification, replacing ducted gas heating with reverse-cycle electric heat pumps is an emerging activity. VEEC eligibility applies where an existing gas heating system is replaced with an eligible high-efficiency electric alternative.

**Key requirements:**
- Gas disconnection or decommissioning evidence required
- New system must meet efficiency rating minimums
- Activity is growing rapidly as gas prices increase and Victorian new-build gas bans take effect

### 4. Appliance Activities

The appliance category is where a lot of electrician work now sits, and it is the one most VEU guides skip. It covers installing approved high-efficiency appliances in residential and non-residential settings — induction cooktops (the big one, given the gas-to-electric push), refrigerators and freezers, clothes dryers, televisions, in-home displays and pre-rinse spray valves — plus high-efficiency motors in non-residential settings. Where the appliance installation involves wiring work, the installer must be a licensed electrician registered with Energy Safe Victoria.

### 5. Commercial and Industrial Solar PV

Easily the largest certificate value per job on the activity list: commercial and industrial solar PV systems under the program carry advertised discounts of up to $34,300 on a 200 kW system. This is **Activity 47**, and it runs from 30 kW to 200 kW — below 30 kW there is no VEU claim, above 200 kW there is none either.

**This is the one activity where the 2026 rules changed in your favour, and most guides have not caught up.** The federal Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme was expanded from 100 kW to 1 MW for systems **installed on or after 1 October 2026**, which means a commercial job in that band can now create **STCs as well as VEECs from the same installation** — previously anything over 100 kW was locked out of STCs and eligible only for large-scale generation certificates. The two certificates are created under different schemes, by different parties: the VEEC through your VEU accreditation or that of the accredited provider you work with, the STC through the Clean Energy Regulator.

Two timing details that decide whether a job actually gets paid. First, the SRES expansion carries a **fixed five-year deeming period** to 2030 for systems between 100 kW and 1 MW, rather than the declining deeming period that applies under 100 kW. Second, the CER has said STC applications for mid-scale systems **will not open until mid to late November 2026** — so a system installed on 1 October is eligible, but nobody can lodge the STC paperwork yet. Do not promise a customer an STC payment date before applications open.

There is a choice buried in this, not just a bonus: systems between 100 kW and 1 MW can take STCs **or** participate in the Renewable Electricity Guarantee of Origin for REGO certificates, and the CER warns that eligibility under one scheme may affect eligibility under the other. Run both numbers before you specify the job. The STC option pays upfront; REGOs pay on generation and suit a system owner who keeps the asset.

### What is *not* an activity any more

Two things this page used to list — **hot water pipe insulation** and a standalone **draught sealing** activity — are not current VEU activities. Draught sealing did not disappear, though, and this is worth knowing if you have been quoting it: it now sits inside **activity 15, weather sealing**, which is residential-only and still live. The catch for electricians is a training condition — activity 15 requires the *Weather Sealing Safely with Gas Appliances* module, and while licensed electricians are otherwise exempt from the program's mandatory training units, that module is not one of the exemptions. The published activity list is: commercial and public lighting, water and space heating and cooling, appliance activities, space conditioning and shower rose activities, project-based activities, other activities (gas efficiency and cold rooms), commercial and industrial solar PV, and ceiling insulation. If a guide lists an activity that isn't on that page, check the ESC activity guide before you build a quote on it.

## Step-by-Step VEEC Claim Process

### Step 1: Activity Selection

Confirm the job qualifies. Check the relevant Activity Definition on the ESC website for the specific activity type. Verify the product is on the approved products list. Confirm the customer's property type, existing system details, and location (VEU is Victoria-only — a property in NSW does not qualify).

### Step 2: Customer Agreement

Obtain written consent from the customer to assign VEECs to you (or your scheme participant). This is a non-negotiable evidence requirement. Most aggregators provide standard assignment forms — if running your own accreditation, create one based on ESC guidance.

### Step 3: Installation

Complete the installation to the relevant standard. For electrical work, all installations must comply with **AS/NZS 3000:2018 (Wiring Rules)** — specifically **Clause 1.5** (fundamental principles), **Clause 1.8** (verification — inspection and testing) and the mandatory test methods in **Section 8, clause 8.3**. For heat pump hot water, follow manufacturer specifications and state plumbing codes where applicable.

### Step 4: Evidence Collection

Collect the required evidence before leaving the site — you cannot return to remedy missing documentation without putting the VEEC claim at risk:

- Photos of the **old product** being removed (including serial number or rating plate where visible)
- Photos of the **new product** installed (including serial number, rating plate, installation location)
- Commissioning documentation
- Signed customer assignment form
- Any required test results or compliance certificates

### Step 5: Certificate Creation

Log into the VEU Registry and create the VEEC claim. Enter site details, product details, and upload evidence. The registry calculates the number of VEECs from the activity rules. Each certificate attracts a **$4.35 creation fee**, invoiced on Tuesdays and Thursdays; if you spot a problem you can withdraw the claim before 6am on the invoice day to avoid the fee, and claims are then assessed on a risk basis before registration.

**The deadline that actually bites: VEECs must be created no later than six months after the end of the year in which the activity was completed.** Work done in 2026 must have certificates created by **30 June 2027**. Miss it and the abatement is simply gone — there is no late lodgement and no appeal. Registered certificates expire six years after the activity date.

### Step 6: Certificate Trading

Once certificates are created and validated, they appear in your registry account. You can:

- **Sell directly to retailers** (requires establishing relationships — larger volumes needed)
- **Sell via brokers** (immediate liquidity, small brokerage fee, typically 3–8%)
- **Sell via aggregators** (if working under aggregator accreditation — price agreed upfront)

Certificate prices are market-driven. Monitor spot prices via industry sources or the ESC market data before committing to forward sales.

## VEU and Solar STCs on the Same Property

This is where Victorian electricians often get confused — and where errors can create compliance problems.

The **VEU program** and the **federal STC scheme** are separate, parallel programs administered by different regulators:

| | VEU | STCs |
|---|---|---|
| Regulator | Essential Services Commission (ESC) | Clean Energy Regulator (CER) |
| Scope | Victoria only | National |
| Applies to | Energy efficiency upgrades | Renewable energy generation |
| Certificate type | VEEC | STC |
| Trading market | VEEC market | STC market (SWH/SGU) |

**The key rule:** VEECs and STCs are not mutually exclusive on the same property, but they may apply to different activities in the same visit.

**Example scenario:** A Victorian household has a 6.6kW solar system installed. The customer also wants to replace their old electric storage hot water with a heat pump unit in the same job.

- The **solar system** generates **STCs** under the federal Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme (SRES), administered by the **Clean Energy Regulator (CER)**
- The **heat pump hot water** system generates **VEECs** under the **VEU program**, administered by the **ESC**
- Both sets of certificates can be created from the same site visit — they're independent schemes for different activities

**VIC-specific gotcha:** Heat pump hot water systems can generate VEECs *or* STCs in some configurations — but not both for the same installation. STCs for heat pump hot water systems are created under the SRES's solar water heater (SWH) category. If you assign STCs, you cannot also create VEECs for the same hot water installation. Choose the higher-value path before installation — run both calculations.

In 2026, VEECs for heat pump hot water are generally higher value per installation than SWH STCs for the same unit. Run the numbers for each specific job using the ESC VEEC calculator and the CER STC calculator before advising the customer.

## How to Build VEU Into Your Business

### Pricing VEU jobs correctly

The VEEC value should inform your customer pricing, not just your margin. Most accredited persons offer customers a **point-of-sale discount** equivalent to the VEEC value — making the installation cheaper for the customer while maintaining or improving your net margin. This is the standard commercial model for heat pump hot water under VEU.

Work the example backwards from the advertised discount, because that is the number the customer has already seen. The program advertises **up to $630 off a heat pump hot water system**. At an $83 certificate, $630 is about seven and a half VEECs. If your calculator says twenty for the same job, one of the inputs is wrong — the climate zone, the system being replaced, or a product that isn't on the ESC register. The calculator's number is the one to quote; the marketing figure is a ceiling, not a target.

This is a genuine competitive differentiator against non-accredited competitors quoting the same job at full price.

The biggest time cost in VEU participation is evidence management. ### Volume requirements for direct accreditation

The ESC's accreditation process requires ongoing compliance — annual fit and proper and competent and capable tests, a biennial independent assurance audit at your cost, and six years of records. The fixed cost is real and knowable: **$4,000 in fees in year one** ($3,000 application plus $1,000 renewal) before you create a single certificate, plus $4.35 per certificate after that. So the decision is arithmetic rather than instinct — if the certificate value you'd capture on your own volume doesn't clear about $4,000 a year plus the admin time, working as a registered scheme participant under someone else's accreditation is the better trade. Above 10–15 eligible installs a month, direct accreditation usually clears that bar comfortably.

## Common VEU Compliance Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Installing a product not on the approved list | VEEC claim rejected; no certificate revenue | Check ESC approved product list before quoting every job |
| Missing pre-installation photos | Claim cannot be validated | Photograph old system before removing it |
| Customer assignment form not signed | Certificates cannot be legally created or traded | Obtain signed form before installation, not after |
| Claiming VEECs and STCs on the same HWS installation | Non-compliance; potential certificate cancellation | Choose one scheme before the install |
| Missing product serial numbers in evidence | Claim queried or rejected | Photograph rating plate with serial number clearly visible |
| VEU claim on a non-VIC property | Claim void | Confirm address postcode before quoting VEEC value |
| Certificates not created by 30 June of the following year | Abatement lost permanently — no late lodgement exists | Create claims as jobs close, not in a year-end batch |
| Renewal lodged inside 90 days of expiry | $7,376 late fee; if it lapses, the full $3,000 application again | Diarise the expiry 120 days out |
