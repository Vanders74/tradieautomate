A data centre electrician in Australia is a licensed electrical tradesperson who installs, commissions and maintains the power path inside a data centre — switchboards, UPS systems, busway, PDUs, and increasingly the high voltage distribution that feeds AI racks. They are covered by state electrical licensing and, on construction sites, by the Work Health and Safety Act 2011. The role is not a separate trade; the pay premium comes from what the site demands, not from a different certificate.

**The short answer:** advertised data centre electrician roles in Australia clustered between **$47 and $65 an hour** in October 2026 — roughly **$93,000 to $128,000** on a 38-hour week. Shift-based and commissioning roles commonly package **$110,000 to $160,000**, and HV-qualified commissioning electricians in Sydney are being quoted higher.

## What the award actually pays

There is no data centre award. These workers sit under the **Electrical, Electronic and Communications Contracting Award MA000025**, made under the Fair Work Act 2009 and administered by the Fair Work Ombudsman.

The relevant classification is **Electrical Tradesperson Grade 5** — a senior tradesperson who supervises, plans and coordinates the work of others.

| Classification | Hourly | Weekly (38 hrs) | Annual (52 wks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ET Grade 1 | $29.45 | $1,119.10 | $58,193 |
| ET Grade 3 | $32.13 | $1,221.10 | $63,497 |
| ET Grade 5 (senior) | $34.46 | $1,309.50 | $68,094 |

Those figures are the minimum. They exclude the industry allowance ($41.41/week), the licence allowance ($40.29/week for an unrestricted licence), tool allowance, overtime and shift loadings. Our full [electrical award rates guide](/blog/electrical-contractor-award-rates-australia-2026/) lists every classification and allowance.

The gap between the award floor and what data centres advertise is the whole story: **$34.46 becomes $47–$65**.

## What data centre roles actually advertise

Two independent readings of the market in October 2026:

**SEEK listings.** A count on 18 October 2026 found **637 data centre electrician roles** nationally, with advertised base rates clustering **$47–$65/hour**. On a 38-hour week that is **$92,872–$128,440**, before shift loadings and site uplifts.

**Recruiter market reports.** Recruiters tracking the same market in October 2026 reported **861 SEEK roles**, advertised rates of **$55–$68/hour**, annual packages of **$120,000–$190,000** including overtime and loadings, and Sydney commissioning electricians clearing **$150,000+** before sign-on bonuses.

**Modelled rate bands.** Poachd's August 2026 model put the Sydney data-centre-grade electrician band at **A$62–$95/hour base**, benchmarked against ETU/NECA enterprise agreement scales. That is a model, not a survey — treat it as a ceiling indicator, not a quoted rate.

**Why the range is so wide.** These are not the same job:

- **LV fit-out** — cable pulls, tray, terminations, testing. At or near the bottom of the band.
- **Commissioning** — proving systems against a script, documenting results, integrating with the controls contractor. Mid to upper band.
- **HV switching and isolation** — writing and executing switching programs, holding a switching authority. Top of the band, and the scarcest skill.
- **Shift maintenance** — 24/7 rosters with night loadings. Lower base, higher package.

## Why the premium exists

Four things drive the rate, and none of them is "data centres pay well because they're big".

**1. HV authorisation.** Most large sites distribute at high voltage and step down inside the building. An electrician who can be authorised to switch HV is a different risk category from one who cannot, and contractors price that accordingly.

**2. Commissioning discipline.** A data centre is validated, not just built. Work is tested against scripts, results are recorded, and change control is enforced. Electricians who can produce clean commissioning documentation are worth more than those who only install.

**3. Uptime consequence.** An outage in a live data centre is measured in dollars per minute and contractual penalties. That risk transfers into wages through roster design, permits and supervision ratios.

**4. Security and onboarding.** Background checks, site inductions and access controls under the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 mean contractors carry non-productive onboarding time. Sites pay for certainty of supply.

## Contractor rates: the maths, not a number

If you are subcontracting rather than employed, the advertised hourly rate is not your charge-out rate. Build it:

1. **Base wage** — say $55/hour for an experienced data centre electrician.
2. **On-costs** — add 25–30% for superannuation, workers compensation, leave, allowances and PPE. That is roughly $69–$72/hour true cost.
3. **Overhead and margin** — multiply by 1.6–1.8. That gives roughly **$110–$130/hour** charge-out before site-specific costs.

Then add what data centre work actually carries: higher public liability cover, induction and training time, documentation, test equipment calibration, and the supervision ratio the site requires. A domestic charge-out rate will lose money on this work.

Price the scope where you can — terminations, testing, commissioning support, remediation — and keep hourly rates for variations.

## State differences

**New South Wales** carries the deepest market because Sydney has the largest concentration of hyperscale and colocation builds. It is where the top of the advertised band sits.

**Victoria** is the second market, driven by Melbourne's data centre corridor, but the volume of roles is smaller and rates typically sit below Sydney at the same skill level.

**Queensland and Western Australia** have smaller data centre footprints, so the premium is less consistent — but the same HV and commissioning skills transfer to mining and resources work, which sets a competing floor for wages.

The practical implication: the premium is a **site-density effect**, not a state award difference. The award is national; the market is not.

## How to get in without data centre experience

The licence gets you eligible. The site gets you hired. Three routes that work:

**Go through a subcontractor already inside the fence.** Electrical contractors who hold the head contract need licensed bodies. Joining them as a subcontractor or employee is the fastest way to accumulate site hours and commissioning exposure.

**Build the adjacent evidence.** HV awareness, testing and inspection experience, structured cabling, and the ability to work to a documented procedure. If you have run compliance-heavy work — solar commissioning, switchboard upgrades, industrial maintenance — say so in those terms.

**Target the construction phase first.** Fit-out work has a lower bar than live-site maintenance because the environment is not yet operational. It is the natural entry point to commissioning.

What will hold you back: no HV exposure, no commissioning record, and no evidence of working under change control. Fix those before applying to a live-site role.

## The business case, not just the rate

A data centre contract is usually larger, longer and more documentation-heavy than domestic work — which means it changes the shape of your business, not just your hourly rate.

Recurring maintenance and commissioning support are the durable parts: they repeat, they are contract-based, and they are what a buyer values when you eventually sell. If you are using data centre work to grow, price the contract for margin and recurring scope, not just to win the first job.

## FAQ

### How much do AI data centre electricians make in Australia?

Advertised data centre electrician roles clustered between $47 and $65 an hour in October 2026, which is roughly $93,000 to $128,000 a year on a 38-hour week before overtime and shift loadings. ### What is the award minimum for a data centre electrician?

There is no separate data centre award. Data centre electricians are covered by the Electrical, Electronic and Communications Contracting Award MA000025. An Electrical Tradesperson Grade 5 — a senior tradesperson who supervises and coordinates work — is paid $34.46 an hour, or $1,309.50 a week, under that award. Advertised data centre rates run roughly 35% to 90% above that floor.

### Do you need high voltage (HV) authorisation to earn the top rates?

On most sites, yes. HV switching authorisation is the single biggest rate differentiator because it lets you operate and isolate high voltage distribution, not just work on low voltage equipment. Contractors pay a premium for electricians who can hold a switching authority, write switching programs and supervise HV isolation. Without it you can still get data centre work on LV fit-out, but the ceiling is lower.

### Is data centre work shift work?

Much of it is. Operating data centres run 24/7, so maintenance and commissioning roles often involve rotating shifts, night work and on-call rosters. Shift loadings, overtime and site uplifts are how a $47–$65 base becomes a $110,000–$160,000 package. Construction-phase work is usually Monday to Friday with overtime rather than rotating shifts.

### Can a domestic or solar electrician move into data centre work?

Yes, but the gap is specific: HV awareness, commissioning and testing discipline, structured cabling, and site security processes. Employers hiring for data centres want evidence you can work to a commissioning script, document test results and follow change control. A domestic background gets you the licence; the transition usually comes through a subcontractor already inside the fence rather than a direct hire.

### How should a contractor price data centre subcontract work?

Start from the true employee cost, not the advertised rate: base wage plus 25–30% for super, workers compensation, leave and allowances. Multiply that by 1.6–1.8 to cover overhead, risk and margin, then price the scope (cable pulls, terminations, testing, commissioning support) rather than the hour alone. Data centre work carries higher insurance, inductions, PPE and documentation costs than domestic work, so a domestic charge-out rate will lose money.

### Do data centre electricians need a security clearance?

Site access requirements vary by operator, but most major data centres require background checks, photo ID, induction and in some cases citizenship or security clearance for restricted areas. Critical infrastructure obligations under the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 also flow down to contractors working on designated assets. Budget time for onboarding — it can take weeks.
