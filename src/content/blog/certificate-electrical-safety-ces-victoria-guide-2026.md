## What Is a Certificate of Electrical Safety?

A Certificate of Electrical Safety is a document that:
1. Records that electrical installation work was carried out and tested by a licensed electrical worker
2. Certifies that the work complies with the relevant Australian Standards and the Electricity Safety Act 1998 (Vic)
3. Is lodged with Energy Safe Victoria, giving the regulator a record it can audit

The COES system is set up by the **Electricity Safety Act 1998 (Vic)** and the **Electricity Safety (General) Regulations 2019 (Vic)**, which also set the certificate fees. ESV is responsible for issuing, selling, accepting and managing the certificates.

**Key principle:** The COES is not a paper formality. It carries the worker's certificate of compliance — a signed statement that the work complies. Signing that for work that doesn't comply is an offence in its own right, separate from failing to lodge the certificate.

---

## When Is a COES Required?

A COES is required for **all electrical installation work** in Victoria. There is no "notifiable" threshold and no minimum job size — ESV's position is that the Act and the General Regulations 2019 require a certificate for all electrical installation work.

"Electrical installation work" is broad, and covers:

- Installation or alteration of electrical wiring in a building
- Connection of electrical equipment to fixed wiring
- Installation, replacement, or alteration of switchboards or switchgear
- Solar PV system installation (including all grid-connect and battery installations)
- EV charger installation (as a fixed electrical installation)
- Air conditioning installation
- Swimming pool electrical installation

What changes with the job is the **type** of certificate and the **deadline**, not whether one is needed:

- **Prescribed** — work that must be inspected by an independent Licensed Electrical Inspector before it is connected or used. This includes consumer mains, main switchboards, meter boxes and main earthing, and it is where most domestic solar and battery work lands.
- **Non-prescribed** — general work like additional power points, light fittings and repairs. No independent inspection, but the certificate is still mandatory.

There is also a **periodic COES**, which covers a rolling 3 months of non-prescribed work for a single responsible person — useful for maintenance contractors, and not available electronically through ESVConnect.

Whatever the job, the safe assumption is that a certificate is required. The cost of issuing one you weren't strictly obliged to issue is trivial next to the penalty for not issuing one you were.

## Who Can Issue a COES?

**A Registered Electrical Contractor or a licensed electrical worker.** The COES is not restricted to inspectors — ESV is explicit that RECs and LEWs (A Grade electricians and Restricted Electrical Licence holders) purchase and complete certificates and give a copy to their customer. Unregistered and unlicensed people cannot buy or lodge certificates at all.

The work splits into three signed parts:

1. **Certificate of compliance** — completed and signed by the licensed electrical installation worker who did the work, within 4 business days of finishing. This is where the accountability sits, and it forms part of the COES.
2. **Certificate of inspection** — only for prescribed work, completed by an independent Licensed Electrical Inspector, who also has 4 business days after the inspection to give it to the person responsible.
3. **The COES itself** — completed by the responsible person (the REC, or a licensed worker acting as one) and given to the customer and to ESV.

**The employee question:** an employed electrician *does* sign the certificate of compliance for their own work — that is the statutory duty in s44 and it can't be delegated to the office. What an employee cannot do is trade for a customer in their own right: in Victoria a licensed worker must work for, or be registered as, an REC to perform work for profit. So on a multi-crew solar job, the installer on site signs the certificate of compliance, the inspector signs the certificate of inspection, and the REC completes and lodges the COES.

## The Two Deadlines (It's Not 28 Days)

There is no 28-day rule in Victoria. Section 45A(6) of the Electricity Safety Act 1998 sets **two different clocks**, and which one applies depends on the type of work:

| Work type | Clock starts | Certificate due |
|---|---|---|
| **Prescribed** (mains, switchboard, solar, batteries) | When the inspector gives the certificate of inspection | **4 business days** |
| **Non-prescribed** (power points, lights, repairs) | When the certificate of compliance is completed | **1 month** |
| **Periodic COES** | End of the 3-month coverage period | **4 business days** |

Both clocks must land the certificate with **the customer and Energy Safe Victoria** — not one or the other. The certificate of compliance itself is also due within 4 business days of finishing the work (s44(2)), which is what starts the non-prescribed clock.

In practice, treat 4 business days as the working deadline for anything prescribed. A solar job where the inspector signs on Friday and the office batches certificates at month-end is already in breach by the following Thursday.

For solar installations, the COES should be issued after:
- The system installation is complete
- The system has been inspected and tested
- The inspector has issued the certificate of inspection (prescribed work)
- The installer is satisfied the system complies with AS/NZS 3000 and AS/NZS 5033

For battery installations, add: compliance with AS/NZS 5139 (battery energy storage systems).

## What Must the COES Include?

A valid Victorian COES must include:

**Certificate of compliance (signed by the worker who did the work):**
- Name, licence number and REC registration number of the responsible person
- Address where the work was performed
- Description of the work performed — specific enough to identify what was installed
- Date the work was completed
- Statement that the work complies with the Act and the regulations

**Certificate of inspection (prescribed work only):**
- The Licensed Electrical Inspector's name and licence details
- Date of inspection
- Statement of whether the work complies

**Certificate details:**
- Date of issue and the certificate reference number
- The REC registration the certificate was purchased under

**Equipment details (for solar/battery):**
- For solar PV: panel brand, model, quantity, inverter brand/model, system capacity
- For battery: battery brand, model, capacity, installation location

**The more detail, the better.** Vague descriptions like "electrical installation" don't meet the requirement to describe the work, and they are the first thing an auditor will query — because the auditor is comparing your description to what is physically on the wall.

---

## The Network Notification Requirement

For solar installations, the CES obligation exists alongside (but separate from) the **network notification requirement** — the obligation to notify the relevant distribution network service provider (DNSP) of the new solar installation.

The network notification must be made using the relevant DNSP's process. In Victoria, this is typically via the DNSP's online portal (AusNet Services, Jemena, United Energy, CitiPower, Powercor depending on the area).

**Common mistake:** Thinking the COES satisfies the notification requirement. It doesn't — they are separate obligations to separate bodies. You need to:
1. Lodge the COES with the customer and Energy Safe Victoria (via ESVConnect for electronic certificates) within the deadline for the work type
2. Notify the DNSP separately (typically before or at time of connection)

## How to Issue a COES in Victoria (2026 ESVConnect Fees)

Energy Safe Victoria's portal — **ESVConnect** — is the only place electronic COES can be purchased and lodged. Paper certificates still exist, but they are bought from authorised electrical wholesalers and can only be lodged through the IVR line on 1300 360 366, not through ESVConnect.

The electronic process:

1. Log in to ESVConnect (register if not already registered, using your licence or REC registration)
2. Purchase the certificate — prescribed or non-prescribed
3. Enter the installation address and work details
4. Enter your REC registration number
5. Complete all required fields including equipment details for solar/battery
6. Submit — the certificate is lodged electronically

**Keep a copy.** After lodging, download and store a copy against the job record. If ESV or a property owner queries the certificate later, you need to be able to retrieve it immediately. ESV does not amend certificates once generated, so errors are corrected by cancellation and reissue.

**COES fees for FY2026–27** (from 1 July 2026; no GST — the purchase of a COES from ESV does not attract GST):

| Certificate | ESVConnect | Paper |
|---|---|---|
| Prescribed | **$39.70** | $43.20 |
| Non-prescribed | **$9.00** | $10.00 |
| Periodic (3 months, non-prescribed) | not available electronically | $984.40 |

You can pass the purchase price on to the customer — s45B(12) and regulation 265 of the General Regulations 2019 allow it — but you cannot add a margin to it.

**What a late or missing certificate actually costs.** There is **no late lodgement fee**. ESV does not bill you $150–$300 for a late certificate; the exposure is an infringement notice or a prosecution under s45A of the Act:

| | Individual | Body corporate |
|---|---|---|
| Infringement notice (6 / 30 penalty units) | **$1,254.60** | **$6,273** |
| Maximum in court (60 penalty units) | **$12,546** | — |

At the 2026–27 penalty unit value of **$209.10**. Failing to have prescribed work inspected at all is a separate and heavier offence — 120 penalty units ($25,092) for an individual, 600 penalty units ($125,460) for a body corporate under s45(1).

## Common CES Audit Failures — What Triggers ESV Action in 2026

Energy Safe audits completed installation work against the certificate that describes it, and it already holds your lodgement record. These are the failure patterns that most commonly turn an audit into action:

**1. Missing or late COES lodgement**
The most common failure — and the most easily avoided. The clock runs from the inspection (prescribed) or the certificate of compliance (non-prescribed), not from invoice, customer sign-off, or grid connection approval. Contractors who batch certificates at month-end miss the 4-business-day window every time jobs cluster.

**2. Signing a certificate of compliance before the work is complete**
The certificate of compliance is a signed statement that the work complies. Signing one for work with punchlist items outstanding, or for work you haven't tested, is an offence under s44(3) — 60 penalty units — and is treated as a disciplinary matter by ESV that can escalate to suspension or cancellation of the licence.

**3. Vague or generic work description**
"Solar installation" or "electrical work" does not describe the work. The certificate has to be specific enough for an auditor to know what was installed and check it. Adequate format: *"Installation of 22× 440W Jinko Tiger panels (9.68kW), Fronius Primo 10kW inverter, 10kWh BYD HVS battery, AC connection to existing Clipsal 3-phase switchboard, dedicated circuit and protection relay installed."* Include brand, model, capacity, and connection type for every major component.

**4. Getting the three signatures mixed up**
The installer signs the certificate of compliance for their own work. The inspector signs the certificate of inspection for prescribed work. The responsible person completes the COES and gives it to the customer and ESV. Sub-contracting is where this breaks: if a solar company engages a licensed sparky, the sparky still signs the certificate of compliance for the work they did — it is not the office's signature to make.

**5. Confusing the COES with DNSP notification**
Lodging the COES with ESV satisfies your ESV obligation. It does not notify your DNSP (AusNet, Jemena, CitiPower, Powercor, or United Energy) of the grid connection — that is a separate requirement. Both obligations must be met independently.

**6. Battery storage labelling gaps**
Solar + battery systems require more documentation than PV-only work. The COES must cover the battery system electrical installation, and labelling requirements under AS/NZS 5139 must be completed and documented. Missing battery warning labels, non-compliant signage, or absent energy management system documentation are consistently flagged in ESV audits of battery installations. See our [AS/NZS 5139 compliance guide](/blog/as-nzs-5139-battery-storage-compliance/) for full labelling requirements.

**7. No copy retained and no copy given to the customer**
ESVConnect records the lodgement, but the obligation is to *give* the certificate to the customer and to ESV — the portal record is not a substitute for the copy. Owners who sell or refinance without a COES on record will pursue the installing contractor, often years after the job.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance (2026 Penalties)

Failure to issue a required COES, or signing an incorrect one, is an offence under the Electricity Safety Act 1998:

- **Infringement notice:** 6 penalty units as an individual (**$1,254.60**), 30 penalty units for a body corporate (**$6,273**), per certificate
- **Prosecution:** up to 60 penalty units in court (**$12,546**) for an individual. Skipping the inspection on prescribed work is heavier again — 120 penalty units, $25,092
- **Licence action:** ESV can take disciplinary action that results in suspension or cancellation of a licence. That is a separate consequence from the fine, and for most contractors it is the expensive one
- **Insurance implications:** work that isn't correctly certified can create gaps in professional indemnity coverage
- **Civil exposure:** if an installation the certificate described causes a fire or injury, a certificate that overstates what was done is evidence against you

The penalty unit value is reset every 1 July, so the dollar figures above move annually — the penalty unit counts (6, 30, 60, 120) are the fixed part.

## NSW Equivalent: CCEW

For electricians working in NSW, the equivalent document is the **Certificate of Compliance — Electrical Work (CCEW)**. It is issued under the *Gas and Electricity (Consumer Safety) Act 2017* and administered by **Building Commission NSW** — not SafeWork NSW, which handles WHS and licensing but has no role in electrical compliance certificates. NSW also moved to eCert as the lodgement channel. See our dedicated CCEW NSW guide for the full details.
